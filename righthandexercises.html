Right Hand Exercises

The necessity of breaking down a complex problem into components is nowhere better seen than in developing the right hand. It also shows the advantages of focusing on basics and the way that a systematic approach can save time.

Five fingers - six strings. These are the components that must interact in guitar playing. How they interact must be understood, and one must be able to deal with all possible combinations in order to be prepared to play anything that may arise.

From the simple to the complex: how one finger interacts with a string is the starting point. Now, the actual mechanics are difficult to describe in writing, and really must be learned from a teacher, but certain principles can be enumerated: keeping the thumb out in front of the fingers, motion from the knuckle, followthrough, relaxation.

Fingers move in towards the palm, striking a string during the motion, and then return to the rest position. These two motions are made by different muscles, and so must be developed equally. (Take a look at Grey's Anatomy, or any equivelent text, to make sure you understand how the mechanism of your hand works). When you strike a string, you exercise those muscles against the resistance of the string, and overcomming that resistance will develop strength and speed. When returning to the starting position, however, the finger moves throught the air and encounters no resistance. It will not, therefore, become as strong or fast in that motion. The only action which exercises the extensor muscles against resistance is rasgeado, and this is (I suspect) one of the reasons why flamenco guitarists have such fast and strong technique- their action is more balanced due to constant rasgeado practice. Even if one does not play flamenco music, one can benefit from this kind of practice.

Rest your thumb on the sixth string, and strum downwards with the index finger. You have just exercised your index finger extensor muscle against resistance. Now strum upwards with the same finger, and repeat the motion. Feel the finger moving against the strings in both directions, moving from the knuckle. Do this with the middle, ring and pinky. (Even though the pinky is not used in "classical" guitar playing, strengthening it will help balance the hand. If your pinky sticks out while you're playing, it's because the muscles are too weak to control it, plus it's too tense. Strengthening it will correct this.) You can either count the downstrokes, doing, say, a hundred repetitions, or you can time each finger, doing the motion for a minute or more. Other rasgeado patterns are also good to practice, especially if you want to play some flamenco music (and there's no reason why you shouldn't), but this basic exercise will give you the technical benefits.

Next, each finger can play a note rest stroke, or free stroke, or something in between. Do these motions with each finger separately. This is the way to make sure the action of each finger is cool and especially that placement is accurate. If you can play repeatedly with one finger and stay relaxed, alternating with two or more fingers will present no problem. Generally one practices either full rest-stroke or free-stroke, but there are actually many useful gradations in between. Start by playing rest-strokes with one finger, then gradually begin to shift towards free stroke so that several repetitions are somewhere in between. Go back and forth. Make sure you treat the thumb as an equal to the other fingers: practice them all the same amount.

Next comes combinations of two fingers at a time, and this also introduces the difference between both fingers playing the same string, and each finger playing a different string, as in arpeggio. These are the basic two-finger combinations:

Rest Stroke: im, ia, ma
Free Stroke: pi, pm, pa, im, ia, ma

Notice that the combinations with the thumb are considered equal in every way to the combinations using just the fingers. The goal is to develop a balanced technique in which all fingers are equal. These combinations can be practiced on just an open string, on one string with some kind of simple ascending chromatic scale pattern, or on a regular scale. In addition, they should be practiced with each finger playing a different string. This arpeggio action is different enough so that it requires its own practice. Instead of a featureless string of imimimimimimimim...., use different accents: groups of two, three, four or more. Practice "bursts": play a group of notes as fast as you can, then stop and relax your hand completely. Repeat. A good grouping is five notes: four as one beat and the fifth as the next beat, so that the fifth note is the goal of rhythmic motion. Be radical: use a clock and play im rest-stroke on one open string without stopping for 5 minutes, for 10 minutes. What does this develop that other exercises do not? In many ways, im rest-stroke is the foundation of right hand technique: these two fingers are used the most, they are in the center of the fingers that are used, and rest-stroke develops the most strength.

Three fingers at a time. Now there are more permutations:

Rest stroke: ami, ima, amim
Free stroke: pim, mip, pimi, pia, aip, piai, pma, amp, pmam, ima, ami, imam

"ami" is important. Practice it this way:

a m i a m i a m i a m i a m i a m i a m i a m i....etc
>------->------->------->------->------->----

In other words, accent in groups of four, so each fingers gets the accent in turn. Practice the free-stroke combinations as arpeggios as well as on one string.

Four at a time:
Free stroke: pima amip pimami pmiamaim piami (flamenco tremelo pattern)
Be sure to do these on one string as well as as arpeggios. Start each permutation with each finger in turn, so the accent isn't always in the same place: pima, imap, mapi, apim.

The Villa-Lobos Etude #1 pattern is also fundamental: practice this alot. Also, tremelo is fundamental. Practice this alot with the thumb playing rest-stroke; this stabilizes the hand.

Scales are more "advanced" exercises in that they involve both hands. It is useful to investigate each hand separately before combining them, and that is what these right hand exercises do.
